How a deputy is built

Four layers, each doing one job.

The layers are separate on purpose. The part that talks cannot act on its own, the parts that act cannot send, and all of it leaves a record.

  1. 01

    The deputy

    The one voice people hear

    Only your deputy talks to people: on the phone, on WhatsApp, by text. It answers what is simple itself and hands the rest to its squad.

  2. 02

    The squad

    Specialists, each with its own tools

    A specialist looks things up, keeps records and drafts, with its own tools and nobody else's. It cannot send. Every message leaves through one gate, as your deputy, once.

  3. 03

    The workflows

    Known work, drawn as steps

    A piece of routine work is a drawn graph: who does each step, what each step is given, and what counts as done. Steps can run side by side.

  4. 04

    The record

    Every job, kept

    Each job is one durable row: which tool ran, what it returned, what was drafted, who approved it and when. A job is never silently run again.

How one job runs

Six things happen to every job.

This is the part a demo never shows, and the part that decides whether you can rely on it.

RoutedA quick, cheap choice first: one specialist, a known workflow, or the squad together when the work spans several.
BudgetedEach job runs to a deadline and a spending cap. If it reaches either, it stops and hands over what it has.
Checked in codeNo model marks its own work. A figure must trace to a tool's result and a total must be the sum of the records. One more try with the failure spelled out, then it is handed over as partial.
ReviewedAnything a customer will see becomes a draft that waits for one of your named people.
TracedEvery step is kept: which tool ran and what it said. “Where did that figure come from?” always has an answer.
DrilledThe squad is drilled like software is tested: scripted jobs graded by code and by a second model, run on every change and against the live models every night.

Smart Call Handling

The phone, handled. Three ways.

The phone is one door, and for many small businesses the one where work is lost quietly: after hours, on a busy line, in a voicemail nobody checks. Your deputy covers for your team and backs it up. It takes no one's place.

Out of hoursLive

Every call answered.

When you close, your deputy picks up: evenings, weekends and bank holidays. It answers from your own business information, takes proper details and promises a call back, which waits for you as an item due at your next opening.

Busy lineLive

No caller lost to a busy line.

Your phone provider forwards a call to your deputy when you're engaged or don't pick up, so customers keep the number they know. The note is on your WhatsApp before you've finished the job in hand.

Put throughLive

Through to you, when you're there.

In hours, a caller who asks for you hears a short hold line while your mobile rings. A briefed transfer, where you hear who is calling and why and press 1 to take it, is built and in testing.

What it handles on your line

  • Answers from your own hours, services and facts
  • Follows your standing rules, written in your words
  • Knows a returning customer: the last call, the notes, what was promised
  • Greets your staff by name and takes work from them
  • Starts the workflow the call needs: an estimate, a search, a call back
  • Asks before texting your customer what was agreed
  • Puts a caller through to a named person
  • Says so when it doesn't know, and takes a message

Safeguards, not optional extras

Callers are told they're speaking with an AI assistant, and that the call is recorded, in the opening sentence. Your deputy doesn't quote a price or commit you to anything on a call. When it lacks the facts it says so and takes a message.

Inbound-first, by design

Your deputy answers the calls your customers make to you. It rings out only when one of your people asks it to, on a brief they have read and approved, one call at a time. No cold calling and no dialling lists, which keeps you square with Ofcom's rules and keeps your number's reputation clean. Ofcom guidance →

Approvals

Every workflow stops where a person should decide.

Every draft has a short name, like P14. You answer it the way you would answer a colleague, from WhatsApp or by text. Or tap Approve, Drop or Change it on your own pages.

01

A named person approves it.

Anything a customer will see waits at review until one of your people says ok.

02

Your ok covers what you read.

Change anything and it comes back as a new version, to be approved again.

03

Every message goes once.

If we can't tell whether one was delivered, a person decides before it is tried again.

04

Outside text can't give orders.

A caller's words or an email can inform a draft. They can never make your deputy send or change anything.

These four are rules in the code, each held by its own automated test. They are not instructions we hope a model follows.

ok P14Accept the draft, as the version you read.
drop P14Bin it. Add a reason if you like; it is kept.
show P14See it again, and how to answer it.
P14 labour is 2 hoursSay what to change. A new version comes back for your ok.
resend P14Try again a send that failed. Only a person can.
sendSend your latest email draft as it stands.
remember that…Keep a fact or a rule. It holds on every job from then on.
new workflow: when…Describe a workflow. Your deputy draws it for you to turn on.
